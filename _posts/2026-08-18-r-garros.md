title: "Most Teams Aren't Failing. They're Just Not Tenacious."
---

Every failed transformation gets blamed on the wrong thing: bad tooling, bad timing, or bad buy-in.

The real reason is simpler and less comfortable. The team expected the work to be easy, and it was not.

Roland Garros has a line carved into its walls: *"La victoire appartient au plus opiniâtre."* Victory belongs to the most tenacious. Not the most talented. Not the best resourced. The most tenacious.

Clay courts prove this literally. The surface kills fast winners and rewards whoever can outlast the other side, point after point, for hours. Speed does not decide the match. Endurance does.

Most operational work in a company is a clay court problem. Most leaders keep preparing their teams for a hard court.

A leader announces a migration, a platform change, or a process overhaul. The plan looks clean in the deck. Then week three hits: tickets pile up, a key stakeholder pushes back, someone finds an edge case nobody scoped. This is not a sign the plan failed. This is the plan working normally. But because the team was sold on “smooth,” they read normal friction as crisis, and the initiative loses momentum right when it needed to hold.

The fix is not a better plan. It is a different expectation going in.

**Three things separate teams that hold the line from teams that do not.**

They do not treat friction as evidence of failure. A backlog spike after a migration is data, not a verdict.

They measure recovery speed, not incident count. Every team has outages. The ones worth learning from are the ones who get back to baseline fast and can say exactly why.

They protect the boring middle. The first week of a change gets attention. The fifth week, when it is just unglamorous cleanup, is where most initiatives quietly die. That is the week that decides the outcome.

None of this is inspiring. That is the point. Tenacity is not a mindset you talk about in a kickoff meeting. It is what remains after the mindset wears off and the work is still there.

**Do this next time something breaks:** skip the retro question “what went wrong.” Ask instead, “where did we expect this to be smooth when we should have expected it to be hard.” That question tells you more about your team’s actual maturity than any postmortem template will.
