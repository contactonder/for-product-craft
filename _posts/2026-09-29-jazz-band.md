Something in product work just inverted, and most teams are still operating as if it hasn't. For decades the scarce resource was execution. Now it is judgment. Teams that haven't noticed the swap are still gatekeeping the wrong thing.

Product advisor Ravi Mehta, previously CPO at Tinder and a product leader at Facebook and TripAdvisor, has a name for the shift: moving from an assembly line with distinct risk gates to a jazz band where the entire team riffs off of each other. It is a good metaphor. It is also worth pressure testing before we build an argument on top of it.

## The Friction That Was Actually Doing a Job

The old product pipeline (research to ideation to spec to design to build) was not slow by accident. Every handoff was a checkpoint that protected an expensive, scarce resource: engineering time. You couldn't afford to build the wrong thing, so you spent weeks making sure you were building the right one first.

AI didn't remove a bottleneck. It removed the reason the bottleneck existed. Writing code, drafting specs, generating tickets, all of it now moves at machine speed. Talking to customers, aligning stakeholders, and deciding what actually deserves to exist still move at the same human speed they always did. Mehta recently gave a talk at Ford, the company that put the assembly line into industrial production a century ago, on exactly this point: the sequencing that made sense when building was expensive stops making sense the moment building is nearly free.

## From Minimum to Lovable

The old core question was "is this worth building." That question made sense when building was the expensive part. The question that actually matters now is "is this worth shipping," and it is a harder one to answer, because there is no longer a resource constraint forcing you to answer it early.

| Old Paradigm | New Paradigm |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Execution capacity** | **Human judgment** |
| **Minimum Viable Product** | **Most Lovable Product** |
| **PM as gatekeeper** | **PM as curator** |
| **"Is this worth building?"** | **"Is this worth shipping?"** |

A gatekeeper's job was saying no before anything got built. A curator's job is saying no after several things already exist, side by side, and only one of them deserves to survive contact with a real user.

## Where the Metaphor Actually Breaks

Here is the part worth being honest about. A jazz band doesn't start as a jazz band. Every musician in it spent years alone with one instrument before they were good enough to improvise with anyone else. "Everyone plays every instrument now" isn't what happens when a rhythm section gets AI tools. What actually happens looks more like what a development consultancy called Whitespectre found when they audited a prototype a single PM built in four hours using AI coding tools: only 30 percent of the code was usable in production. Speed without a filter doesn't produce a jazz band. It produces a lot of untuned instruments playing at once.

That is the real argument for keeping a curator role at all. Abundant output doesn't remove the need for judgment. It just moves judgment from "should we build this" to "which of these five things we already built deserves to exist."

## Taste Is a Filter, Not a Feeling

"Taste" gets used a lot in this conversation, usually as a stand in for something nobody can quite define. That is a problem, because a team cannot build toward a quality it hasn't named.

Taste, stated plainly, is a fast, specific filter for what your particular users will actually love, applied to a pile of outputs that all technically work. It is not a personality trait some people have and others don't. It is a set of criteria a team can write down, argue about, and get better at applying, the same way any other judgment call gets sharper with practice and feedback. Teams that treat taste as ineffable end up making the call by vibes in a Friday demo. Teams that treat it as a skill build an actual rubric: what does lovable mean for this specific product, and how would we recognize it in five competing prototypes.

## What Actually Survives

AI didn't take the craft out of product work. It removed the slow, expensive scaffolding that used to hide how much of the job was always judgment. The assembly line protected teams from having to answer the hard question early. The jazz band doesn't protect anyone from anything. It just makes clear, immediately, who in the room can actually tell a good idea from a merely working one.

That was always the job. It is just no longer possible to hide behind a six week build cycle while you figure out whether you have it.
