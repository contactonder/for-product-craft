It happens in almost every product pitch and strategy meeting. Someone puts up a slide with a giant circle worth billions of dollars, points to it, and says: "If we get just 1% of this, we are golden."

Leadership nods. Marketing gets excited. The project gets approved.

The circle is called the TAM, or Total Addressable Market: the yearly revenue you would earn if every possible customer bought your product. It is an easy way to make an idea look huge on paper. But it skips the hardest part of the work: who will actually buy, and what it costs to win them.

I want to take market size seriously before I criticize it. It is useful. It tells you how high the ceiling is, and in an earlier post on the ICP I wrote that growth is limited by the ceiling of the segment you choose. But a ceiling is not a plan. TAM answers "Is the prize worth chasing?" It does not answer "Can we win it?"

Two checks answer the second question: ICP precision and growth leverage.

## The Circles Inside the Circle

To make the math look more careful, presenters nest smaller circles inside the TAM. The SAM, or Serviceable Addressable Market, is the part your product and sales reach can serve. The SOM, or Serviceable Obtainable Market, is the part you could realistically win.

It looks disciplined. But the filters are usually chosen from the top: "We will target mid-market technology companies in North America." Nobody has shown that those companies want to buy. Splitting an unproven number into three pieces does not make it proven.

A big number also anchors the room. Anchoring means the first number in a discussion pulls every later number toward it. Once a ten-billion-dollar circle is on the screen, a plan for one million dollars of revenue sounds small, even when it is realistic.

So build from the bottom instead. Start with what your team can actually do this quarter. Imagine three sellers. Each runs about ten serious sales conversations a month. One in five becomes a customer, at fifteen thousand dollars a year. That is six new customers a month, or about one million dollars of new yearly revenue after twelve months. *(These numbers are an illustration, not a benchmark.)*

Next to the one hundred million dollars that "1% of ten billion" promised, this is about ninety times smaller. That gap is not a failure. It is information. It shows how much has to change before the slide becomes true: more sellers, a higher close rate, a higher price, or a product that sells itself.

## The Customer Hidden Inside the Number

The standard TAM formula multiplies the number of accounts in an industry by an average price. It assumes every account has the same problem, the same budget, and the same willingness to change.

That blurs your ICP, the Ideal Customer Profile. *(The ICP describes the company that gets the most value from your product.)* A Director of Operations at a mid-sized company and a VP of Finance at a large enterprise may sit in the same TAM bucket. But their problems, buying cycles, and onboarding needs are very different. If your math treats a 50-person startup and a 10,000-person enterprise as equal slices of one pie, your plan will fail.

So shrink the focus. Define the exact type of customer who has an urgent problem and a real budget today. A market of five hundred million dollars full of urgent buyers is often worth more to a young company than a market of fifty billion dollars full of passive observers.

## The Question the Slide Never Asks

A big number says nothing about what it costs to win those dollars. A market can be worth billions, but if your CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost, what you spend to win one customer) eats your margin, the size does not matter.

This is where growth leverage comes in. By leverage I mean this: each customer you win makes the next one easier or cheaper to win. Your product fits so cleanly into a painful workflow that selling, expanding, and keeping customers become steps you can repeat.

A very large TAM can signal a crowded market with many established competitors. So ask where your wedge is. *(A wedge is the narrow entry point that lets you win a first group of customers.)* How does winning your first ICP help you win the next group? If reaching your TAM means adding sales reps, ad spend, and support staff in a straight line, you do not have a TAM strategy. You have an expensive treadmill.

This also links to the way you sell. In an earlier post, [Is Raising ACV the First Question?](https://contactonder.github.io/for-product-craft/acv/), I argued that a larger cheque only makes sense if the way you sell can support it. The same is true for market size. A bigger market needs a sales motion that can reach it.

## Two Questions to Ask Instead

The next time a team shows concentric circles to justify a project, do not argue about whether the market is ten billion or fifty billion dollars. Change the conversation:

1. **Who is the clear, specific ICP inside this market that we can win this quarter?**
2. **What leverage do we have that makes winning and keeping them cheaper and faster than it is for our competitors?**

Glitter looks impressive on every slide, because it catches the light. Gold is different. It is heavy, and you only hold it after you have done the work. Answer these two questions with precision, and the market math becomes a result, not a promise.
