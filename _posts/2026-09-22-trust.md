title: 'Is Building Trust a Form of Manipulation?'
---

There is a question most practitioners have quietly thought but nobody seems willing to ask out loud.

Every framework for building trust in influence without authority roles looks, from a certain angle, like a manual for performing care rather than having it. Ask more questions. Remember personal context. Share credit visibly. Suppress the appearance of self-interest. If you follow that advice deliberately, in service of an outcome you want, are you building trust or manufacturing the appearance of it?

I want to take that question seriously rather than dismiss it.

## The Framework That Provoked It

The Trust Equation, from Maister, Green, and Galford's book *The Trusted Advisor*, expresses trust as a ratio:

**Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Orientation**

Credibility is the perception that you know what you are talking about. Reliability is the perception that you do what you say. Intimacy is the perception that you are a safe person, meaning what someone shares with you will be handled with care.

Self-Orientation sits in the denominator for a structural reason. Even a small increase in perceived self-interest dramatically reduces trust, regardless of how high the numerator is.

The prescription follows: demonstrate expertise, follow through, create safety, and visibly subordinate your interests to theirs.

Sound advice. Also, if you hold it at arm's length, a fairly precise description of what a skilled manipulator would do.

## Where the Critique Holds

If someone applies the Trust Equation as a tactical playbook, asking questions to appear curious, sharing credit to signal generosity, and suppressing self-interest to seem other-focused, then they are manufacturing the conditions of trust in service of an agenda the other person cannot see. The behaviours being identical to genuine care does not make them genuine care.

The consulting world, where this framework originated, is not innocent here. Intimacy as technique has a long history in client relationship management: remembering names, asking about weekends, and showing up as a whole person rather than a billable resource. Often genuine. Also, in many firms, explicitly trained as a retention tool. The line between a real relationship and a performed one is genuinely blurry, and the financial incentive to blur it is significant.

The manipulation critique has real teeth. Dismissing it is lazy.

## Where It Gets Complicated

Most people who are naturally trusted do not experience their behaviours as strategic. They ask questions because they are curious. They share credit because hoarding it does not occur to them. The Trust Equation describes what they do; it did not instruct them to do it. The framework is descriptive of something that already exists in high trust people, not a script for manufacturing it.

Two things happen when someone tries to adopt the behaviours without the underlying disposition.

First, it usually does not work. People are remarkably adept at detecting performed intimacy over time. The colleague who asks questions but never changes their position based on the answers is performing curiosity. Most people eventually notice, and the trust collapses faster than it was built.

Second, behaviour frequently produces the disposition it was initially imitating. In social psychology, Daryl Bem’s Self-Perception Theory (1972) demonstrated that individuals infer their own internal attitudes by observing their own overt actions. Acting less self-oriented, consistently and over time, forces your internal disposition to align with your habits. Identity is shaped by behaviour as much as behaviour is shaped by identity. The external technique and the internal character trait are not as separable as the manipulation critique assumes.

## The Test That Actually Matters

Professional relationships involve mixed motives. You care about colleagues and you want to advance your career. You want the product to succeed and you want credit for it. You are genuinely interested in a stakeholder's perspective and you are trying to influence their decision. Insisting that motives must be purely other-oriented to be legitimate sets a standard nobody meets.

The question that does real work is not "am I using a technique?" It is this: **if the person I am building trust with fully understood my motives and the long-term outcome I am working toward, would they feel deceived?**

Call it the transparency test.

The test is not whether they agree with every decision in the moment. Teammates frequently disagree on tactics, priorities, or trade-offs. An engineering partner might resist a difficult refactoring decision today, but if they understand your shared commitment to product excellence, that relationship survives full disclosure. You have interests, they have interests, and the trust serves both.

If you are building intimacy with a stakeholder to close a decision they would resist if they understood your full position and true motives, that relationship does not survive disclosure. That is manipulation, and no framework changes what it is.

The Trust Equation cannot draw that line. You have to draw it yourself.

## Why This Is Sharper for PMs

A manager using trust-building techniques has institutional structure underneath the relationship. Trust supplements authority.

For a PM, trust does not supplement authority. It is the authority. There is nothing else.

That changes the stakes of the manipulation question considerably. When trust is your only lever, the temptation to instrumentalise it is structurally higher than it is for people with formal power. And performed trust, sustained at the intensity influence without authority requires, is exhausting in a way genuine investment is not. You cannot maintain the performance indefinitely. When it slips, the trust built on it collapses faster than trust built on real care.

The most durable version of influence without authority is built on actually caring about the people you work with, actually wanting the product to succeed, and actually being willing to let someone else take the credit when that is what the situation calls for. That is not a naive position. It is a strategic one, because it is the only version that compounds.

The Trust Equation describes what trust looks like from the outside. The Transparency Test gives you an operational way to audit it. But whether the practice remains genuine or degrades into manipulation is decided by what you are willing to expose when the cards are on the table.
