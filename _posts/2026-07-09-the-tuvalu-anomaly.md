title: "The Tuvalu Anomaly: Why AI Still Needs Human Judgment"
---

In my last piece, I recommended the Nielsen Norman Group study guide for user psychology. While reviewing their video on **[Usability Heuristic 6: Recognition vs. Recall in User Interfaces](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/recognition-vs-recall/)**, something felt off.

The instructor explained that asking "Is Lisbon the capital of Portugal?" is easier because it relies on recognition. Asking "What is the capital of Portugal?" requires harder recall.

On paper, it was perfect. So I tested it with an advanced AI model. The AI gave a confident, textbook perfect explanation.

Then I challenged it: "Is Funafuti the capital of Tuvalu? Think hard."

The machine immediately corrected itself.

Here is the lesson. If a user has never heard of Tuvalu, no amount of beautiful typography will help them recognize Funafuti. They are left guessing.

This small test reveals a big trap in the AI era.

AI excels at summarizing frameworks, generating polished answers, and sounding authoritative. But it often lacks real world context, cultural awareness, and the ability to pressure test ideas against unpredictable humans.

If I had trusted the AI without question, I would have published a flawed idea. It took human critical thinking to spot the gap and find the deeper product lesson.

We now live in a world where answers are cheap and instant. This makes human judgment more valuable than ever.

The real skill is no longer knowing the frameworks. It is the discipline to question them, challenge the consensus, and keep your own critical thinking in the drivers seat.

Never outsource your judgment to a machine.
