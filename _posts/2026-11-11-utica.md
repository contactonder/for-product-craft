In 1867, a literary anthology printed a poem with the line “I wrote 2 U B 4.” Read it aloud and it says: “I wrote to you before.”

It is a text message, written more than a century before mobile phones. In 2010, the British Library announced an exhibition on the English language that included it. The Guardian covered the news with a joke in the headline: textspeak is “soooo 19th century.”

The poem is fun. But the better story is what happens when you look at how people react to it, and how they reacted to language change in every era before it. Those reactions are biases. Product teams have the same ones, and they shape what we build.

## The First Example Is Never the First
The 1867 poem did not come from the compiler of that book. It had been printed in American publications since at least 1847. An earlier version, written for a woman called Catherine Jay of Utica, appeared in 1832. And in 1828, a London magazine published two tragicomic verses about Ellen Gee of Kew and Emily Kay of Ewell, with their names written as letters. Later, a researcher on a language mailing list pointed to an American example from 1813.

Each time, the origin moved earlier. The same research links this kind of abbreviation play to the birth of “OK” in the 1830s, which began as a joke spelling of “oll korrect.” The linguist David Crystal argues that the same creative impulse shaped textspeak.

This is the first lesson for product teams. The first example you find is not the first example that existed. It is the one that survived and got noticed. When a team says “nobody has ever done this,” the honest next step is to look for an older version.

## Bias One: The Recency Illusion
Linguists call it the recency illusion: the belief that something you noticed recently must also be recent.

Textspeak feels like a mobile phone invention. It is not. People have compressed language for speed, play, and belonging for at least two centuries.

Product teams make the same mistake. They say: “Users no longer read. They want shortcuts.” Or: “Writing short prompts to an AI tool is a new skill.” In both cases, an old need is shown in a new interface. Keyboard shortcuts, slash commands, and chat abbreviations are the same human habit as the Victorian poem.

The danger is practical. If you believe a behavior is new, you design for a new type of user. If you see that it is old, you design for a durable need.

## Bias Two: Declinism, and Who Gets Called Clever
Declinism is the belief that things were better in the past and are getting worse.

The Guardian notes that Victorian abbreviation poems were called emblematic poetry, and people considered them terribly clever. The same trick done by the text generation is often called a sign of decline. The exhibition itself shows that this reaction is old. In 1712, Jonathan Swift argued that English was in chaos and that a group of experts should fix it for good. The British Library said that debates about whether language is changing, declining, or improving come up again and again.

The behavior is the same. The judgment changes with who does it, and when.

Here is how it appears in product teams. Imagine that every Friday, many customers export their data to a spreadsheet to build a weekly report. A team in decline mode says: “Our users do not understand the product.” A team that looks closer says: “Our product is missing a report.” A workaround is not a training problem. It is one of the most honest forms of user research you will ever receive.

## Bias Three: When Fear Rewrites the Rules
The Guardian also describes a Victorian pamphlet called *Poor Letter H*. It told lower middle class readers who wanted to get on in life to say “house” and not “’ouse.” The British Library’s curator of sociolinguistics says that the H we hear in words like “hospital” today exists because of this anxiety about being judged. In the curator’s words, people were afraid of being punished for dropping an H.

This is close to what linguists call hypercorrection: overcorrecting out of fear of being wrong. The fear did not follow logic. It changed the language itself.

Users and buyers do the same. They over approve. They ask for every certificate. They copy the choice that nobody could blame them for. In my earlier post on the ICP, I wrote about career risk, the fear that a person who picks a new vendor will be blamed if it fails. It is the same force. Before you ask what a user wants, ask what they are afraid of.

## Three Questions Before You Judge a User Behavior
You cannot remove bias from a team. But you can ask better questions before you decide what a behavior means:

1. **Is this behavior really new?** Look for an older version. If you find one, the need behind it is probably durable.
2. **Who is judging it, and why?** The same shortcut is “clever” when we do it and “lazy” when a customer does. Check whether the label says more about the observer than the behavior.
3. **What is the person trying to get?** Four needs are worth checking first: speed, play, belonging, and safety.

## The Message Arrived
The Victorian reader who saw “I wrote 2 U B 4” had no trouble understanding it. The message arrived for everyone who looked past the format.

Product teams face the same choice with every new behavior. You can judge the spelling, or you can read the message. Your users have been writing to you before. The question is whether you are reading.
