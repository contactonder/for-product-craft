There is a quiet trap that catches growing software companies right around the middle of their journey.

The product works. A small team somewhere finds it, installs it on a credit card, and uses it every day. The metrics look clean enough to build a forecast. Then the growth curve flattens.

The founding team looks at the revenue model, panics slightly, and reaches for the standard playbook: move upmarket, larger logos, enterprise cheques.

They treat the ICP like a dial. ICP means Ideal Customer Profile: the description of the company that gets the most value from the product. They change the company size filter in the CRM from fifty employees to five thousand. They update the pitch deck with blue corporate styling. Then they wait for the enterprise to arrive.

It often fails, and the reason is simple. Moving upmarket is not a marketing change. It is a product change, a sales change, and an organisational change. It can work, but only when you treat it that way.

You do not just sell to a bigger company. You sell to a different buyer, who buys for a different reason and fears different things.

## The Lever of Who You Sell To
Growth is rarely limited by how well you build features for your current users. It is limited by the ceiling of the segment you have chosen to serve.

When a company starts out, it usually sells to a small team. The workflow is close and personal. A handful of developers, marketers, or founders use the product because it removes an annoying problem from their Tuesday afternoon. The buyer and the user are the same person, or they sit three desks apart. The sale is self serve: the customer signs up and pays without talking to a salesperson. A credit card is enough.

Moving from a team tool to enterprise trust is one of the stronger scaling levers a software business has. Every step up changes the rules. Five things move together:

- **The trigger:** why the customer is in the market at all.
- **The buyer:** who decides the purchase.
- **The fear:** what they are afraid will go wrong.
- **The proof:** what evidence they need to see.
- **The motion:** how the sale and packaging are actually run.

The rest of this post walks through them.

## Situational Triggers
A situational trigger is the event that pushes a customer to look for a solution now, instead of next year. An enterprise does not buy because the landing page is sleek or the feature list has two more checkmarks. It buys because something reached a breaking point.

When a small team adopts a messaging tool, the trigger is simple: email threads are messy, and the team wants speed. The software solves an annoyance.

Slack’s move into Enterprise Grid required a different trigger. A large company was not losing sleep over chat speed. It was dealing with administrative challenges like unmanaged shadow IT (employees using tools the IT department never approved), data sitting in many countries with no residency controls, and the inability to enforce retention or run discovery across the company. What unlocked the enterprise cheque was not a faster composer. It was admin controls, compliance features, and the ability to govern many workspaces under one layer.

If the product only answers the small team trigger, its architecture will not survive the enterprise one. You cannot market your way out of that mismatch. The trigger decides what the software has to withstand.

## The Moment the Fear Becomes Real
As you climb, the user and the economic buyer separate. The economic buyer is the person who controls the budget and can approve the invoice.

For a small team, the buyer is usually an individual contributor or a team lead. Their mood is closer to optimism. They want to work faster, build better things, and feel proud of their craft.

For an enterprise, the buyer is often a security leader, a procurement lead, or an operations director. Their mood is closer to caution. They are not trying to feel faster. They are trying not to be the person who approved the thing that later caused a problem.

Here is what that looks like in practice. A founder receives the first serious prospect’s security questionnaire: two hundred questions about encryption, data location, access controls, and incident response. Nobody on the team knows how to answer half of them. The deal that looked certain last week sits in a folder for six weeks. The product did not get worse. The company simply discovered that it had never been asked to prove trust before.

That folder is the new buyer’s fear made concrete. The fears are wider than security alone:

- **Security and compliance risk:** a data leak, or a missed regulatory requirement.
- **Career risk:** if the new vendor fails, the person who chose it will be blamed. For many buyers this is personal, and often stronger than any technical worry.
- **Implementation risk:** a rollout that takes a year, disrupts teams, and never reaches full adoption.
- **Vendor survival risk:** whether the company will still exist in five years.
- **Committee risk:** large purchases are decided by groups. In those groups, one person without the power to say yes may still have the power to say no. That person is a blocker.

A small team asks, “Will this slow me down?” An enterprise asks, “What happens if this goes wrong, and who will be responsible?”

If you sell to that buyer with a small team pitch, you will lose. You talk about speed while they weigh liability. For this buyer, the decisive capabilities are often the ones the original users barely notice: the audit log, the permission hierarchy, and the SOC2 report. SOC2 is a security audit standard that many enterprise buyers require before they sign.

## Proof, Motion, and Packaging
Architecture is only half of the work.

Proof means the evidence a cautious buyer can show to their own boss: security reviews, audit reports, and references from similar companies. A good trial is no longer enough.

An SMB product can be sold by the user. An enterprise product is sold to a committee that includes security, procurement, and legal. If you keep the self serve motion and only add an audit log, you still lose. The person who loves the product cannot sign the order. The person who can sign will not buy until the motion matches how they buy: a security review, negotiated terms, a contract that procurement can process.

Packaging has to move with them. The cheap plan that converted the original team will not carry the enterprise cheque, and the enterprise edition cannot be a higher price on the same bundle. It has to be the place where the new fears are answered. If those answers sit in the base plan, you have given the valuable work away.

## The Cost to the Base
Climbing is not free. Deals that closed in days now take months. Security, legal, and support functions that did not exist become necessary. Those costs are expected.

Climbing the ICP and raising ACV are closely linked. In an earlier post, [Is Raising ACV the First Question?](https://contactonder.github.io/for-product-craft/acv/), I argued that a larger cheque only makes sense if the way you sell can support it. The same is true here. A bigger buyer needs a bigger sales motion behind the product.

The sharper cost is the one that is easy to underestimate. The small customers who built the business can feel forgotten while the roadmap fills with enterprise requests they will never use. Governance features can slow the core workflow. Sales motions that work for the security leader do not work for the team lead. The product becomes more defensible and less used by the people who proved it worked.

Climbing is the right response when the new trigger is real and the original job still holds for the people who will keep using it. It is the wrong response when the stall means the original job was narrower than the forecast assumed, and the enterprise version would require abandoning the users who got you here. Treating every flat growth curve as a signal to move upmarket is how companies destroy the thing that was working.

## The Sequence
You do not begin at the enterprise level if you started as a team tool. Trying to build enterprise trust before you have solved a daily workflow for a small team is like building a house before you have chosen the land. Some products, particularly in security or infrastructure, start with the enterprise fear and never have a clean small team motion. That is a different path. For products that began with a team, the sequence is the opposite.

Start where the friction is sharpest and the feedback is fastest. Stay only as long as the original job continues to expand inside that segment. When growth stalls, look at the problems your users face as they grow, and ask whether a new trigger has appeared that a different buyer will pay to remove.

Study that trigger. Listen to the new fears, including the ones that are not about security. Rebuild the architecture, the packaging, and the motion together. Decide, explicitly, how you will keep serving the customers who got you here.

Do not climb because the company size filter is easy to change, or because the pitch deck looks more serious in blue. Climb only when the new buyer is already in the market, and only as far as the original job can still survive the climb.
