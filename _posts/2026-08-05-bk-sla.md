I was standing at an airport terminal recently, waiting for my lunch, when a screen behind the counter caught my eye.

It was not showing menu items or ads. It was broadcasting a single operational promise to everyone in line.

On paper, it was a fast food counter. In practice, it was a live service dashboard.

*"Food is leaving our kitchen within 3 minutes average from ordering."*

As someone who spends a lot of time thinking about service delivery, I could not look away.

Technically, the screen shows an internal performance metric: cycle time. It measures the time from when an order hits the kitchen system to when it is handed to the customer.

There is no contract. You cannot sue Burger King if it takes four minutes. By putting the average cycle time on a large screen facing the line, they turn an internal number into a public service level expectation.

### The Psychology of the Public Screen

Why display the internal clock? Because it works on human psychology in three simple ways:

1. **Uncertainty Reduction:** Unknown waits feel longer and more frustrating than known waits. A clear timeframe removes the anxiety of not knowing.
2. **The Anchoring Effect:** Setting the expectation at 3 minutes makes waiting 2 minutes and 45 seconds feel like a win.
3. **Transparency as Accountability:** Publishing the target shows the team is holding itself responsible. Customers shift from impatient observers to informed spectators.

### The Corporate Black Hole

For years, software and IT teams have treated concepts like incident management, MTTR, and SLAs as technical jargon reserved for engineering rooms.

Meanwhile, the rest of the enterprise often runs in the dark:

* HR takes a week to process an onboarding request because no one tracks time to first response.
* Legal loses contract reviews in email inboxes because there is no queue visibility.
* Facilities leaves broken equipment unattended because there are no clear escalation paths.

When non technical teams hide behind unmonitored inboxes, they create anxiety. People send endless “just checking in” messages simply because they have no idea when to expect a response.

### Enter Enterprise Service Management

This is why Enterprise Service Management matters. ESM is not about forcing HR or Legal to adopt IT jargon. It is about a simple truth: every internal department is a service team.

Many non IT teams fear SLAs because they sound like rigid, punitive contracts. But the Burger King screen shows that service management is really about transparency.

When a team adopts these principles, three things change:

* **Expectations become clear:** Internal customers know what normal service looks like (for example, “Legal reviews standard contracts in an average of 48 hours”).
* **Bottlenecks become visible:** You cannot improve a process you do not measure. Tracking cycle times exposes gaps quickly.
* **Work becomes proactive:** Teams get alerts before a request breaches its target instead of chasing forgotten emails.

### Service Management for Everyone

Fast food kitchens figured this out decades ago. Managing expectations reduces friction. Measuring cycle times drives consistency.

IT did not invent service management. They just formalized it into software. It is time to stop keeping these principles inside the IT department.

Whether you are flipping burgers, reviewing contracts, or onboarding employees, clear metrics and transparent expectations are not bureaucracy. They are the foundation of good operational craft.
