We love to treat enterprise frameworks like sacred texts.

Organizations take systems like ITIL, COBIT, or COSO, build heavy governance structures around them, and trap themselves in months of analysis paralysis. We wait for the perfect budget, the perfect software, or a complete restructuring before fixing basic operational friction.

It is easy to forget that the best guidance in service management is not new. Tennis legend Arthur Ashe summarized it decades before modern IT frameworks existed:

*“Start where you are, use what you have, do what you can.”*

Whether you are stepping onto a tennis court or managing a chaotic service desk, this single sentence remains one of the most useful operating principles available.

### The Tennis Court and the Service Desk

Arthur Ashe did not win Grand Slams by waiting for perfect weather or opponent mistakes. If you lose the first set, you cannot replay it. You play the current point based on your current state.

That mindset maps directly to the core [ITIL 4 Guiding Principles](https://www.atlassian.com/itsm/itil):

1. **Start Where You Are:** In tennis, you play the ball from where it lands, not where you wish it dropped. In service management, you do not wipe the slate clean. You assess your current reality and build on what already works.
2. **Use What You Have (Keep It Simple and Practical):** A player with modest gear and clear tactics will often outplay someone with expensive equipment and no strategy. Before buying new platforms, maximize the data, talent, and simple workflows already inside the business.
3. **Do What You Can (Progress Iteratively):** You do not win a match with one swing. You win it point by point. Large two-year transformations often collapse under their own weight. Small, continuous improvements create lasting value.

### Why Our Brains Resist Getting Started

This principle is not only practical advice. It also works with how the brain actually functions.

When faced with a massive abstract challenge such as “overhaul our entire governance framework,” the brain’s threat center treats the size of the unknown as high risk. The result is analysis paralysis.

At the same time, the brain prefers to conserve energy. Designing perfect solutions in advance burns significant mental resources.

Arthur Ashe’s approach works with human psychology rather than against it:

* It shrinks the problem. Focusing on what you can do today reduces the sense of threat and keeps problem-solving capacity online.
* It favors good enough over perfect. Humans perform better when choosing a workable option under current constraints instead of searching for an ideal that may never arrive.
* It creates momentum. Action generates small wins. Small wins generate the energy needed for the next step.

### Frameworks Are Tools, Not Religions

You do not need a perfect budget, a perfect timeline, or an ideal tech stack to create progress.

Whether you are managing a service desk, adjusting your approach on the court, or working on a personal goal, the rule stays the same.

Stop waiting for perfect conditions. Start where you are. Use what you have. Do what you can.
