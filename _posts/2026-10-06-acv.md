There is a question product people reach for too early.

How do I make each customer pay more?

It sounds like the grown-up question. It has a clean name, ACV, and a clean playbook: editions, annual plans, add-ons, a higher tier. I want to take the question seriously rather than dismiss it. Raising what a customer pays in a year is a real lever. It is rarely the first one.

## What the Words Actually Mean

SaaS is software people rent. They pay every month or every year, and if they stop paying, the revenue stops.

A logo is a new paying company. Expansion is extra money from companies you already have: more users, a higher plan, another module. An upsell is the same product, more expensive. A cross-sell is a second product. Contraction is a customer who stays but pays less. Churn is a customer who leaves.

None of this is cryptic once the words are tied to a cheque, a customer, or a leak. The cryptic part is the order.

## The System, Not the Slogan

Growth is not a mood. For a subscription product, it is closer to a system:

  Next year's ARR = (New Logos × Win Rate × Year-One ACV) + Expansion − Contraction − Churn
</div>

Five doors. New logos. What they pay at the start. Extra money later. Money that shrinks. Money that leaves.

Every growth idea is someone pushing one door. If you do not know which door is stuck, you will optimise the wrong one. ACV sits in the third term. It is not the machine.

The diagnosis is short:

* **Plenty of trials, weak paid conversion:** The problem is time-to-value, the offer, or the wrong customer.
* **Strong conversion, tiny cheques:** Packaging and target profile are the work. 
* **Decent ACV, retention under 100%:** Do not raise the price. Fix the leak.
* **Strong retention, slow new revenue:** The constraint is distribution or the sales motion, not the cheque.

## Who You Sell to Comes Before What You Charge

ICP means ideal customer profile. Not everyone who could click. The company for whom the pain is frequent, the wallet is real, and the tools already match yours.

The user and the buyer are often different people. The user clicks the product. The buyer can say yes to the invoice. A developer and a salesperson can both use the same tool and still not be the person who signs.

This is often the highest-leverage ACV move that does not require a new feature: sell the same core job to a buyer who can sign a larger cheque. An admin buying a small add-on and a revenue leader buying a view of business risk are not the same purchase, even if the product underneath is similar.

A marketplace is someone else's shop: Atlassian, Salesforce, Microsoft. They take a share of the sale. Co-sell means their sellers help you. Useful, but still not a reason to start with price.

## How You Sell Has to Survive the Cheque

Motion is the style of selling.

Product-led means the product does most of the asking. People try it, reach value, and pay, often without a salesperson. It fits lower ACV, roughly under ten thousand a year, when a user can feel the value without a committee.

Sales-led means a person runs the demo, the security review, and the contract. Buyers expect that once the cheque is large, often above twenty-five thousand, sometimes above fifty.

Hybrid means the product raises a hand and a person answers. That hand is a product-qualified lead (PQL): a company whose behaviour says they are ready. They invited a second team, connected a strategic integration, or hit most of a limit. An MQL only filled a form, which is a much weaker signal.

Raising ACV without changing the motion is how teams stall. They priced like an enterprise product and still sold like a self-serve tool.

A trial ends. A freemium plan stays, with a wall. That wall is a feature gate. It works when the customer has hit a real limit. It feels extractive when you put it there because the quarter is short.

## When the Cheque Is the Right Question

Raising ACV is the right first move when most of these are already true:

1. People reach value, and enough of them pay.
2. Gross retention is not leaking.
3. Power users are sitting on a cheap plan, or enterprise needs are bundled into the base price.
4. You can name the next dollar: a higher edition, a module, a volume band, or an annual commit.
5. You are willing to change how you sell if the new cheque crosses into a sales-led motion.

It is the wrong first move when the customer is still fuzzy, trials never reach value, old customers are shrinking, or you are still losing bake-offs on whether the basic job works. In that last case, the real question is not the price. It is whether you are selling a more valuable job to a more senior buyer. The cheque follows.

Land-and-expand is the sequence, not the hope. Enter with a small, clear plan. Return when the need is larger: more seats, a second team, a second module, or a renewal that can carry a higher edition. Expansion is usually cheaper than a new logo. That does not mean you start by taxing the people who just arrived.

## The Order

The first question is not how to raise ACV. It is which door is stuck, and for whom.

Who is this for, and what job do they hire the product to do? Which constraint moves the yearly number most: volume, conversion, the cheque, or the leak? What should the price grow with? Can the way you sell survive the cheque you want?

Only then: which part of the yearly payment, for which customers, this season.

Pull one lever until it is no longer the constraint. Then ask again. Teams that feast on the acronym and skip the door do not get a larger cheque. They get a slower yes.
